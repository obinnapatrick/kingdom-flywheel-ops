## C25 — Import duty overpaid by UK importers

**What is broken?** When goods enter the UK, the importer pays duty based on a product code, a
value and whether the goods qualify for a trade-deal discount. If any of these is wrong in the
importer's disfavour, they pay too much, and nobody tells them.

**Who cares?** The importer's finance director, if they knew. Nobody else loses anything.

**Why does it cost money?** Overpaid duty is straight lost cash. HMRC will refund it, but only if
the importer claims within three years.

**How big might the problem really be?** Unknown. 251,000 businesses imported goods in 2024, and
about 10% of eligible EU imports did not use a trade-deal discount. But almost half of tariff
lines carry no duty at all, and some unused discounts are not worth claiming. The true
recoverable pool could be large or trivial. **This is the gap that must be closed first.**

**Why hasn't it already been solved?** The person who loses the money cannot see the loss, and
the customs broker is paid per form, not for getting the duty right. Or the pool is small and
not worth anyone's time. We do not yet know which.

**Who is already trying?** Big accounting firms for large importers; customs consultancies; a
handful of new AI-based recovery services.

**Why could we have a wedge?** Mid-sized importers seem to be too small for the big firms and do
not have customs specialists in-house (`HYPOTHESIS`). A no-win-no-fee look-back is easy to say
yes to.

**How would we test it quickly?** First with public statistics only: work out how much duty was
paid on imports that qualified for a discount, by product type and firm size. If that pool is
small, kill it without speaking to anyone. If it is large, the next test is a look-back on a few
importers' own declaration data.

**What would kill the idea?** A small recoverable pool; evidence that audits find more
*under*payments than overpayments (clients would refuse); HMRC rejecting retrospective origin
claims; a crowded, cheap existing market.

**What could become hard to copy?** A record of which refund claims HMRC accepts and rejects, by
product and error type, and a library of the evidence that makes claims succeed.

## C02 — Invalid retailer deductions (US consumer-goods brands)

**What is broken?** Big retailers pay suppliers less than the invoice, citing shortages, damage
or fines. Some of those deductions are wrong, but disputing them is fiddly, so small brands write
them off.

**Who cares?** The brand's controller or CFO: it comes straight off revenue.

**Why does it cost money?** Every invalid deduction is revenue earned and not received.

**How big might the problem really be?** Vendors say 1–3% of wholesale revenue. No independent
study was found. In the UK the grocery code has made the problem smaller.

**Why hasn't it already been solved?** For large brands it largely has been. Smaller brands
cannot justify a specialist.

**Who is already trying?** At least eight specialist software and service firms, plus outsourced
teams. This is the most crowded of the four.

**Why could we have a wedge?** None evidenced. Possibly smaller retailers or distributors the
vendors ignore (`HYPOTHESIS`).

**How would we test it quickly?** Find an independent measurement of deduction rates, and map
which retailers and brand sizes the existing vendors actually cover.

**What would kill the idea?** No independent support for the size, or no gap in vendor coverage.

**What could become hard to copy?** Knowledge of each retailer's dispute rules and win rates,
built up case by case.

## C27 — UK construction subcontractors under-recovering what they are owed

**What is broken?** Specialist subcontractors (electricians, groundworkers, cladders) apply
monthly for payment. Work goes unclaimed, variations are disputed, legal deadlines the main
contractor misses go unused, final accounts drift, and retentions go unchased.

**Who cares?** The subcontractor's owner. Cash flow decides whether the business survives.

**Why does it cost money?** Money earned and never collected, plus the cost of borrowing while
waiting.

**How big might the problem really be?** Retentions alone were £3.2–5.9bn withheld a year, with
£7.8bn unpaid over three years (government research, 2017). How much a typical subcontractor
could recover is unknown.

**Why hasn't it already been solved?** Partly power: subcontractors avoid disputes to keep future
work. Partly capacity: small firms cannot afford a commercial manager.

**Who is already trying?** Freelance quantity surveyors, claims consultants, construction
lawyers; payment platforms such as Payapps that serve the main contractor's side.

**Why could we have a wedge?** Small subcontractors seem underserved (`HYPOTHESIS`), and legacy
retentions and unclosed final accounts are concrete money that can be recovered by looking back.

**How would we test it quickly?** Find independent data on how much applied value is never paid,
and on what freelance QSs charge and recover. A look-back on a few subcontractors' closed
projects would come later.

**What would kill the idea?** Recoverable value per firm too small; subcontractors unwilling to
press claims; the retention ban and late-payment reforms removing most of the pool.

**What could become hard to copy?** A benchmark of how each main contractor actually pays: days to
pay, share of applications cut, dispute behaviour.

## C26 — Out-of-hours electricity waste in UK multi-site buildings

**What is broken?** Shops, offices, gyms and restaurants use electricity when nobody is there,
because heating, cooling, lighting and equipment schedules drift.

**Who cares?** The operations or finance director of multi-site businesses; energy is a large
controllable cost.

**Why does it cost money?** UK business electricity averaged about 24p/kWh in early 2026. Wasted
kilowatt-hours are pure cost.

**How big might the problem really be?** The Carbon Trust says at least 5–10% savings are
available cheaply in every business. That is roughly £2–5k a year for a typical single site, and
more for chains.

**Why hasn't it already been solved?** Spotting the waste is easy. Getting people and building
controls to change, and to stay changed, is not.

**Who is already trying?** Energy consultants, monitoring software, suppliers' portals,
building-controls contractors.

**Why could we have a wedge?** Only if we are paid for verified savings, not for reports, and only
with multi-site operators.

**How would we test it quickly?** Find independent evidence of how long savings last, and whether
multi-site operators pay on a share-of-savings basis.

**What would kill the idea?** Savings that fade within months; operators unwilling to pay for
something they think is free.

**What could become hard to copy?** Benchmarks of energy use patterns across thousands of sites
by building type.

## Why no candidate is selected

The rubric's own rule blocks selection: every shortlisted candidate has L confidence on at least
one of severity, measurability, access or urgency. In all four the weakest link is the same: we
do not know how much money a typical buyer could actually recover. The evidence behind these
numbers is either vendor claims or search-engine summaries of documents we could not open.
